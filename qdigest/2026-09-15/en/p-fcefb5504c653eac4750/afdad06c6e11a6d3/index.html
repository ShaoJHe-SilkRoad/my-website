Can brain stimulation plus cognitive training improve thinking in Alzheimer disease and mild cognitive impairment?

Non-invasive brain stimulation, often shortened to NIBS, refers to methods that act on the brain from outside the body without surgery. The studies in this review paired such stimulation with cognitive training, and asked whether the combination improved overall cognition or particular cognitive domains in people with Alzheimer disease or mild cognitive impairment.

How the evidence was gathered

The team searched five major electronic databases up to 20 November 2023, selected relevant studies, and assessed their quality with standard tools. The combined analysis included 15 studies with 685 patients.

What the combined results showed

Overall, non-invasive brain stimulation plus cognitive training improved global cognition—a broad measure of overall thinking ability—in people with Alzheimer disease and mild cognitive impairment. People with Alzheimer disease also showed global cognition improvement from the combination. A specific form of stimulation, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), combined with cognitive training, produced a benefit as well.

Some benefits were still present at later evaluation follow-up. rTMS combined with cognitive training showed larger therapeutic responses in global cognition, and people with Alzheimer disease had follow-up improvements in global cognition and in attention/working memory. Separately, transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) combined with cognitive training improved language function in people with AD or MCI. The available abstract does not report how long after treatment these follow-up measurements were taken, so the durability beyond that point is unclear.

The authors concluded that NIBS combined with cognitive training, particularly rTMS plus cognitive training, has both short-term and follow-up effects on global cognition, mainly in people with Alzheimer disease. These findings are about measurable cognitive performance; they do not show prevention or cure of Alzheimer disease. The authors also note that more studies are needed to evaluate other specific cognitive domains.

Terms explained

cognitive training

Guided practice on tasks that target specific thinking skills, such as memory, attention, and problem-solving.

The thinking-task practice that was combined with brain stimulation.

Back to the text ↑
mild cognitive impairment

A slight, noticeable change in memory and/or thinking skills but not severe enough to affect the person's ability to complete daily tasks.

One of the patient groups in this review.

Back to the text ↑
repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS)

A procedure where a small coil is placed on the scalp and delivers brief magnetic pulses.

One included brain-stimulation method; when paired with cognitive training, it improved overall thinking scores.
