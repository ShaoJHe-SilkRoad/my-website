A real lift during stroke rehabilitation, but harder to prove later

Yes, with an important qualification. Across 30 randomized controlled trials involving 1,836 adults after a stroke or transient ischemic attack, exercise programmes led to small to moderate improvements in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) by the end of the intervention, compared with the other trial conditions. Those improvements appeared to shrink at later assessments, so the review cannot confirm whether the benefit lasts.

Health-related quality of life is physical and mental health, as perceived by a person or group of people, during a period of time. In everyday terms, it asks how a person feels and gets along day to day after a stroke or a transient ischemic attack, not only how far they can walk or how strong they are. Earlier studies had shown exercise helps physical fitness, walking, and balance after stroke, but data were lacking on whether such interventions lead to improvements in health-related quality of life.

To fill that gap, the authors combined results from those randomized controlled trials in a systematic review and meta-analysis. A meta-analysis is a process that analyzes data from different studies done about the same subject, pooling the results to get a more stable estimate than any single trial could provide. In each trial, participants were divided by chance into separate groups that compare different treatments or other interventions; this random assignment makes the comparison fairer than if people chose their own treatment.

At the end of the exercise programmes, the pooled comparison showed a small to moderate benefit on HRQoL: the improvement was large enough that it was unlikely to be due to chance. At a later follow-up, the benefit appeared smaller, and the result could no longer be confidently separated from no benefit. So exercise improved quality of life while the programme was happening, but the lasting benefit is uncertain.

The benefit was not spread evenly across all parts of quality of life. Exercise improved the physical health domain of HRQoL and the mental health domain of HRQoL to a moderate extent. The social and cognitive composites are also domains of health-related quality of life—how people rate those aspects of their lives—rather than objective performance tests; these showed little difference in the trials.

Some programme features seemed to go along with better results. Interventions that were initiated within 6 months, lasted at least 12 weeks in duration, involved at least 150 minutes per week, and included resistance training appeared most effective. These are patterns observed across the included trials, not a one-size-fits-all prescription for any individual.

Taking these findings together, the review authors concluded that exercise can lead to moderate beneficial effects on HRQoL and should be considered an integral part of stroke rehabilitation. This is their interpretation of the pooled results, not a treatment instruction for any particular person. The main uncertainty is not whether exercise helped during the programme period; it is whether that help remains measurable later. The evidence supports exercise as a valuable part of stroke rehabilitation.

Terms explained

health-related quality of life

Health-related quality of life is physical and mental health, as perceived by a person or group of people, during a period of time.

In this article, it is the outcome the trials measured: how patients felt about their physical and mental health after stroke or transient ischemic attack.

randomized controlled trials

Randomized controlled trials are studies in which participants are divided by chance into separate groups that compare different treatments or other interventions.

In this article, these are the 30 studies whose results the researchers combined.
