Can a positive virus test prove it caused an asthma flare?

No. In this combined analysis of studies, about one in four adults with stable asthma also tested positive for a respiratory virus, so a positive test during a flare is not on its own evidence that the virus triggered the attack.

European respiratory review · 2026-04-08 · Source

New research

Full explanation

A virus test taken during an asthma flare can feel like a smoking gun, but the test detects presence, not cause. The researchers combined results from 111 studies that used molecular methods on respiratory samples from people with asthma, looking at periods when asthma was stable and periods when it was flaring. To avoid stacking the comparison, they included studies that tested participants broadly, rather than only testing people whose symptoms already looked viral, and they excluded studies that selectively tested people for a viral trigger.

In adults, about one in four people with stable asthma (23%) had a respiratory virus. During a flare, that rose to about one in two (50%). Children followed a similar pattern: around one in three when stable and roughly six in ten during an attack. The higher rate during flares is real, but the stable rate is the key comparison: if viruses are already common in non-flare periods, a positive test during a flare cannot by itself say the virus caused it.

A positive result tells you only that viral material was present at the time of the test. It does not show that the infection arrived before the flare, that it produced the airway narrowing, or even that it caused symptoms at all; a positive viral PCR in stable asthma may reflect asymptomatic carriage rather than a true trigger. The authors describe the virus as potentially an innocent bystander, and that is why they conclude a positive test during an acute episode has limited diagnostic meaning on its own.

The main boundary is that this review shows group-level detection rates, not individual timelines. Studies often did not report exactly when samples were taken in relation to the worsening of symptoms, and the pooled results come from different settings and diagnostic methods, creating a lot of variation. So the stable-versus-flare comparison is useful, but it cannot establish which infection preceded which attack or whether treating the virus would have changed the flare. The reasonable conclusion is that a positive virus test is compatible with an asthma flare, but not proof of its trigger.
